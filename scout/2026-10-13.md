## Idea 1: Spend time where Trinity's own losses say it is worth it

**What it is.** Today the clock is shared out by hand-set rules. The
forensics now give us labelled data: moves that were mistakes which more
time would have fixed. The idea is to *learn*, from Trinity's own games,
which situations produce those mistakes, and move time there from moves
where extra time changes nothing.

Steps:
1. For every move in a set of calibration games (wins, draws AND losses,
   so that the model sees ordinary moves too), record what Trinity knew
   when it chose the move: was the opponent's last move the reply Trinity
   had predicted (second move of its previous PV)? how much did the score
   change between the last two completed depths? how many times did the
   best move change during the search? phase (piece count); time spent
   compared with the game's average.
2. Label each move with the forensics method: "fixable mistake" = the
   referee says it lost >= 30 cp AND Trinity at 10x time plays a move the
   referee rates clearly better.
3. Fit a simple model (logistic regression, 5-6 numbers) that predicts
   the chance a move is a fixable mistake.
4. In the engine: multiply the planned time for a move by a factor that
   grows with that chance, then rescale so the *total* time used per game
   stays the same. Time is moved, not added.

**Why it might work.** This is value-of-information from decision theory
and operations research: a fixed budget is best spent where the marginal
value of one more second is highest, and the optimum is where those
marginal values are equal across moves. The forensics suggest Trinity is
far from that optimum: its search mistakes got *less* time than average
(0.75x), so the moves that needed time did not get it. Fitting the rule
to Trinity's own failures, rather than guessing weights, is the
"top-down from results" route that has worked here (data and networks)
where borrowed search tweaks gave ~0. It also targets the unexplained gap
between 10+0.1 (3466) and 40/20 (3480): at the goal time control, time
allocation matters more.

**Cheap test.**
1. Offline, CPU only, no games: build the table from the 1.0.0
   calibration games already on the PC (sample ~5,000 moves, about 10%
   of them from losses). Fit the model and measure how well it separates
   fixable mistakes from ordinary moves (AUC). If AUC < 0.65, stop: the
   signals Trinity has at move time do not predict its mistakes. Before
   fitting, do the noise check ESTIMATES.md already asks for (re-search a
   sample at the SAME time) so that "fixed" is not just randomness.
2. If AUC >= 0.65: SPRT on GitHub, 8+0.08, bounds [0, 5]. If it passes,
   confirm with a 40/20 calibration, because that is where it should
   matter most.

**Prediction.** Self-play +2 to +10. Fraction kept against rated engines:
about 60% (the one earlier time change, +4.8, was never calibrated; time
use helps against any opponent, not only against Trinity).

**Risk.** (a) The labels come from 200 losses; few fixable mistakes per
feature means an over-fitted model. Keep it to 5-6 inputs. (b) A move can
look "fixable" only because 10x time is a lot; the noise check guards
part of this. (c) Trinity's current time rules may already use some of
these signals; then the gain is only from better weights and may be small.
(d) Moving time to hard moves can cause time trouble late in sudden-death
games: keep the existing safety limits unchanged.

## Idea 2: Two equal networks, one team (an ensemble across threads)

**What it is.** With 4 threads, all threads search the same position and
share the hash table. Give the helper threads a *different but equally
strong* network: the averaged network from SPRT #11 (checkpoints
550+600), which scored +0.4 against main. The main thread keeps main's
network. Every thread writes into the shared table, so the main thread
keeps meeting scores computed by a second, independent "opinion" of the
position.

**Why it might work.** Ensemble theory in machine learning
(Krogh and Vedelsby's ambiguity decomposition): the error of a combined
predictor equals the average error of its members *minus* how much they
disagree. Two members of equal accuracy that make different mistakes
combine into something better than either; two identical members gain
nothing. Today all 4 threads share the same blind spots, so the threads
add depth but no diversity. The averaged networks are exactly the case
the theory likes: same strength (SPRT #11 says so with 9000 games), not
the same weights. Biology has the same pattern: genetic diversity makes a
population robust to errors any one member would make. This costs
nothing in speed (each thread runs one network) and only ~25 MB of memory
for the second network. It matters only on the 4-CPU list, which is the
goal line.

**Cheap test.**
1. Offline first (minutes): on ~10,000 positions, compare the two
   networks' evals with the referee (Stockfish, as in forensics). Check
   (a) they disagree by a useful amount (e.g. mean gap > 15 cp) and (b)
   the *average* of the two evals is closer to the referee than either
   alone. If (b) fails, stop: the two networks share their mistakes.
2. If it passes: games at 4 threads on the PC (like the threads test),
   branch vs main, both at 4 threads, 10+0.1, 2 games at a time, SPRT
   bounds [0, 5]. GitHub runners may not have 4 real cores, so the PC is
   the honest place for this.

**Prediction.** At 4 threads, self-play -5 to +8. Fraction kept: about
70% (it is an evaluation-quality effect, and evaluation gains have
transferred well here). At 1 thread: zero by construction.

**Risk.** (a) Scores from two networks differ for the same position, so
the shared table holds slightly inconsistent values; that could make the
search less stable and cost Elo. (b) The 550+600 average may be too close
to main to give real diversity (the checkpoints were trained at a low
learning rate). The bolder 450-600 average is more diverse but scored
-1.3; the offline check decides which to use. (c) Self-play at 4 threads
is slow on one PC: ~1000 games per 3.6 hours, so a decision may take a
day or more of PC time.
