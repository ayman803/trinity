## Idea 1: Mirror twin (test-time averaging of a position and its left-right mirror)

**What it is.** A position with no castling rights and its left-right
mirror image are the same game, so they must have the same value. The
network does not know this exactly; it gives two slightly different
answers. Evaluate both and average them. The average is a free second
opinion from the same network, with no new training.

**Why it might work.** Ensemble theory (Krogh-Vedelsby): the error of an
average equals the members' mean error minus their disagreement, so two
equally good but not identical estimates always average to something at
least as good. Physics and image recognition use the same trick
(symmetrising a measurement; "test-time augmentation"). Cost: the mirror
needs a fresh accumulator, so doing it at every node would be too slow.
Use it only where accuracy matters most and nodes are few: PV nodes and
the leaves reached from the root's top two moves, or whenever the
eval is within a window of alpha/beta.

**Cheap test.**
1. Offline, minutes, no games: take 10,000 positions without castling
   rights (from Trinity's calibration games). Compute eval(p) and
   eval(mirror p). Report the mean gap. If the mean gap is under ~5 cp,
   stop: the network is already symmetric. Otherwise compare, with
   Stockfish at 100 ms as referee (as in forensics), the error of single
   eval vs the average. If the average is not at least ~3% closer, stop.
2. SPRT on GitHub 8+0.08, bounds [0, 5], twin used at PV nodes only;
   second run with twin used near the alpha-beta window.

**Prediction.** Self-play 0 to +6 (a small number of nodes gets a better
eval; speed cost under 3%). Fraction kept against rated engines: ~70%
(evaluation-quality effects transferred well: king buckets 1.1x).

**Risk.** The mirror gap may be tiny (king-bucket mirroring may already
enforce symmetry for most positions). Castling positions have no exact
mirror, so skip them. Mixed twin/non-twin evals in the shared hash table
can be inconsistent; keep the twin to PV nodes first.

## Idea 2: On-policy fine-tuning (train on the positions Trinity actually reaches)

**What it is.** Trinity's net learned from Leela's positions. But the
positions Trinity meets in real games, after its own search choices
against other engines, come from a different distribution. Take the
positions from its own calibration games (about 80 per game; one
calibration is 4000 games = 300k+ positions), label each with Trinity's
own deeper search score (say 10x time, or fixed depth+4), blend with the
game result, and fine-tune the net with this as ~5-10% of the mix.

**Why it might work.** Learning theory calls this covariate shift. In
imitation learning the standard fix is DAgger: collect data from the
learner's own state distribution and have a stronger version label it.
Here the stronger version is Trinity searching more deeply. It also
attacks the "gradual loss" finding: 107 of the 245 tested bad moves were
not fixed by time, which points at systematic evaluation bias in the
kind of positions that arise in real games.

**Cheap test.**
1. Offline calibration (CPU, no training): on 5,000 positions from
   Trinity's own games vs 5,000 from the Leela training file, measure
   mean |static eval - 10x-time search score| (quiet positions only). If
   the game positions are not clearly worse (say <15% higher error),
   stop: there is no distribution shift to fix.
2. If yes: one fine-tune run, ~15-30 min GPU, then SPRT against main.
   Use ONE change only, as ESTIMATES.md advises after the drill failure.

**Prediction.** Self-play 0 to +10 (wide). Fraction kept: ~50% (the data
come from games against rated engines, which should transfer better than
self-play-only data; but similar ideas have gone either way).

**Risk.** The drill network (-92) shows that small fine-tunes on a
selected set can wreck a net. Differences here: positions are unselected,
labels are Trinity's own search plus results, learning rate low and the
mix mostly ordinary data. Labels from its own search can reinforce its
own bias (circular). If the data come only from losses it will be
biased; use wins, draws and losses.

## Idea 3: Winner's-curse discount at crowded nodes

**What it is.** A node's value is the maximum over its moves of noisy
estimates. In statistics the maximum of several noisy numbers is biased
upward ("winner's curse"), by about sigma * sqrt(2 ln k) for k roughly
equal options. So a node with many moves almost as good as the best is
more optimistic than a node with one clear best move. Subtract a small
discount from the returned score that grows with the number of moves
scoring within a margin of the best (counted from what the search already
knows), and is zero when one move stands out.

**Why it might work.** This is a standard bias correction from
statistics (selection bias in extreme-value theory). The current
correction history removes the average bias of the eval at a leaf, but
not the selection bias created by the max at inner nodes.

**Cheap test.** A single parameter (strength of discount, 0 = off). SPRT
on GitHub at 8+0.08 with discount values 1 and 2 cp per extra
near-best move (cap 8 cp), bounds [0, 5]. If either passes, add it to
the SPSA tuner.

**Prediction.** Self-play -2 to +4. Fraction kept: ~40%. Honest low
expectation: searches already average over many nodes, and any effect
is likely small.

**Risk.** Changes scores near mate and draw lines (apply only for
scores within +/-600 cp). Interacts with pruning margins that were tuned
without it; may need a small re-tune. Cheap, so worth one try only after
ideas 1-2.
